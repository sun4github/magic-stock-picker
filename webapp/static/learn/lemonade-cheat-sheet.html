Lemonade Stand — Investor Financial Terms Cheat Sheet

A plain-English reference for every figure the Magic Stock Picker reports, worked end-to-end on one small business.
← Back to the Report Viewer

Executive summary. The three statements work together to show a business's health, and the ratios then judge whether it is cheap and whether it is any good.

  • The Income Statement measures operating performance and profitability on paper, over a period of time.
  • The Cash Flow Statement tracks actual liquidity — money genuinely moving in and out of the bank account.
  • The Balance Sheet is a point-in-time snapshot of assets, obligations and net equity.
  • Valuation & Return Ratios decide whether the enterprise is cheap, expensive, or earning strong returns on the capital it employs.

These figures match the screener exactly

Every formula below is the one magic_formula_starter_screener.py actually computes, so a number you learn here means the same thing when you meet it in a real report. Two conventions are worth knowing up front, because both are deliberate and both differ from a naive reading:

1. Capital Employed excludes cash. Idle money in the tip jar is not capital the business is putting to work, so counting it would understate how efficient the operation really is. The screener keeps only the cash needed to cover current liabilities and strips the surplus.

2. Total Invested Capital is stated two equivalent ways. The screener computes Equity + Debt − Cash; this sheet also shows it as Capital Employed + Goodwill. Expand the first and the cash and debt cancel out, leaving net fixed assets + working capital + goodwill. Same number, two views.

1. Income Statement — the profit tracker

Revenue (Sales)
Total money collected from customers before any cost is deducted.
Formula Quantity Sold × Price per Unit
Example 100 cups × $2.50 = $250.00
Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
Direct cost of the materials that went into what you sold.
Formula Σ (Unit Material Cost × Units Sold)
Example Lemons, sugar, ice and cups = $60.00
Gross Margin (Gross Profit)
What survives after direct input costs. Shows the pricing power of the product itself.
Formula Revenue − COGS
Example $250.00 − $60.00 = $190.00
Operating Expenses (OpEx)
Day-to-day overhead needed to run the business but not tied to any individual cup — rent, advertising, wages.
Formula Selling Expenses + General & Administrative Expenses
Example $30.00 stand rental + $10.00 flyers = $40.00
EBITDA
Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation and Amortization — a proxy for operational cash generation before accounting adjustments.
Formula Gross Profit − OpEx
Example $190.00 − $40.00 = $150.00
Depreciation
A non-cash charge spreading the cost of a long-lived physical asset across the years it will actually be used.
Formula (Initial Asset Cost − Salvage Value) ÷ Useful Life in Years
Example $150.00 stand over 5 years = $30.00 / yr
Operating Income (EBIT)
Earnings Before Interest and Taxes. The true economic profit of the operation once asset wear is accounted for — and the numerator of both Magic Formula ratios.
Formula EBITDA − Depreciation
Example $150.00 − $30.00 = $120.00
Interest Expense
The contractual cost of servicing borrowed money.
Formula Total Debt × Interest Rate
Example 8% on a $100.00 loan = $8.00
Taxes
The government's share of pre-tax profit. Note that EBIT, Earnings Yield and ROC all sit above this line — deliberately, so that differing tax jurisdictions and one-off tax breaks cannot distort which business is genuinely better.
Formula max(0, EBIT − Interest) × Effective Tax Rate
Example A child's lemonade stand pays none: $0.00
Net Income (the bottom line)
Final accounting profit left for shareholders after every cost, including interest and tax.
Formula EBIT − Interest − Taxes
Example $120.00 − $8.00 − $0.00 = $112.00

2. Cash Flow Statement — the liquidity tracker

Operating Cash Flow (OCF)
Cash actually thrown off by daily operations, before reinvestment.
Formula EBITDA − Interest Paid − Taxes Paid ± Δ Working Capital
Example $150.00 − $8.00 − $0.00 = $142.00
Capital Expenditures (CapEx)
Cash spent acquiring, upgrading or maintaining physical assets. Real filings derive this from the change in PP&E; this model uses a maintenance stand-in of 15% of equipment value per year, so a heavier asset base visibly eats free cash flow.
Formula Ending PP&E − Beginning PP&E + Depreciation  (model: 15% of equipment value)
Example 15% of the $150.00 stand = $22.50
Free Cash Flow (FCF)
Discretionary cash left after keeping the existing operation intact. This is what can pay dividends or clear debt.
Formula Operating Cash Flow − CapEx
Example $142.00 − $22.50 = $119.50

3. Balance Sheet & capital structure

Assets
Everything the business owns that carries economic value — cash, inventory, equipment and goodwill. Inventory belongs here: stock counted as working capital is an asset too.
Formula Net Fixed Assets + Cash + Inventory + Goodwill
Example $120.00 + $40.00 + $12.00 + $80.00 = $252.00
Liabilities (Total Debt)
Debts and obligations owed to outside creditors.
Example Outstanding startup loan = $100.00
Equity (Book Value)
Net worth belonging to the owners once liabilities are settled.
Formula Total Assets − Total Liabilities
Example $252.00 − $100.00 = $152.00
Working Capital (Inventory)
Money tied up in trading stock rather than equipment. The screener excludes surplus cash and floors this at zero, so a business financed by its suppliers uses no net working capital at all.
Formula Current Assets − Current Liabilities, excluding excess cash
Example 20% of $60.00 ingredients held as stock = $12.00
Market Capitalization
The aggregate market price of all equity shares.
Formula Share Price × Total Shares Outstanding
Example The market values the stand's equity at $400.00
Enterprise Value (EV)
What it would truly cost to buy the whole operating business: you pay for the equity, inherit the debt, and keep the cash that comes with it.
Formula Market Cap + Total Debt − Cash & Short-term Investments
Example $400.00 + $100.00 − $40.00 = $460.00
Capital Employed
Tangible capital genuinely deployed inside core operations. Cash is excluded on purpose — see the note at the top.
Formula Net Fixed Assets + Working Capital
Example $120.00 stand + $12.00 stock = $132.00
Goodwill / M&A Intangibles
The premium paid above net book value when buying another business or brand.
Example Paying $100.00 for a rival whose physical assets were worth $20.00 creates $80.00 of goodwill
Total Invested Capital
All capital committed to the business, including what was historically paid for acquisitions.
Formula Equity + Total Debt − Cash  (≡ Capital Employed + Goodwill)
Example $152.00 + $100.00 − $40.00 = $212.00, identical to $132.00 + $80.00

4. Magic Formula & return ratios

Adapted from Joel Greenblatt's The Little Book That Still Beats the Market. These ratios separate operating efficiency from capital structure and market sentiment. The method insists on both at once: cheap alone is a value trap, quality alone is an expensive darling.

Earnings Yield (EBIT / EV) — the "is it cheap?" test
Operating earnings relative to the full takeover price. Unlike P/E, it uses Enterprise Value, so a debt-laden company cannot hide behind a flattering share price.
Formula EBITEnterprise Value × 100
Example $120.00 ÷ $460.00 × 100 = 26.09% — higher means cheaper
Return on Capital (ROC) — the "is it any good?" test
How efficiently the operation turns tangible capital into operating profit. A persistently high ROC is the fingerprint of an economic moat.
Formula EBITCapital Employed × 100
Example $120.00 ÷ $132.00 × 100 = 90.91%
ROIC including Goodwill
The same question, but charging the business for what it actually paid in past acquisitions. Read it alongside ROC, never instead of it.
Formula EBITTotal Invested Capital × 100
Example $120.00 ÷ $212.00 × 100 = 56.60%
Free Cash Flow Yield
Cash generation measured against the equity market valuation.
Formula Free Cash FlowMarket Capitalization × 100
Example $119.50 ÷ $400.00 × 100 = 29.88%

5. Quality warning flags

Why a spectacular ROC can be an illusion

Greenblatt's Capital Employed deliberately excludes goodwill, because he is asking what the next dollar put into the business would earn. That is the right question for ranking a basket — but for a company assembled by acquisition it shrinks the denominator dramatically and produces a headline ROC that says nothing about the return earned on the price actually paid.

A real screen by this system flagged FISV at 59.1% intangibles, with ROC reading 136.7% against ROIC of just 9.4% — a 14× gap. An earlier draft of the analysis called that "elite capital efficiency". It was an accounting shadow. These two flags exist so that mistake cannot repeat.

Intangibles Share of Assets
How much of the balance sheet is acquisition premium rather than productive physical assets. Treat anything above roughly 50% as a reason to read ROC sceptically.
Formula (Goodwill & Intangibles ÷ Total Assets) × 100
Example $80.00 ÷ $252.00 × 100 = 31.75%
ROC ÷ ROIC Gap
The multiple between the flattering number and the honest one. Near 1× means the returns are real and earned on capital genuinely invested. A wide gap means the business bought its earnings.
Formula Return on Capital ÷ ROIC including Goodwill
Example 90.91% ÷ 56.60% = 1.61× — healthy
