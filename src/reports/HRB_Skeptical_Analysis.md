## Basic Company Info Questions

### 1. What products or services does the company actually sell?
H&R Block provides assisted income tax preparation (both in-person at retail offices and virtually), do-it-yourself (DIY) tax software (online and desktop), small business financial solutions (bookkeeping, payroll, and payment processing via Block Advisors and Wave), and consumer financial products. These financial products include Refund Transfers, Emerald Prepaid Mastercards, Spruce mobile banking accounts, Refund Advance interest-free loans, Emerald Advance term loans, and tax identity protection services.

### 2. Who are its primary customers?
Its primary customers are individual tax filers in the United States, Canada, and Australia—particularly low-to-middle-income taxpayers, filers needing assistance with tax credits or complex filings, and self-filers using software—as well as small business owners seeking year-round tax and accounting assistance.

### 3. How does the company make money?
The company generates revenue by charging service fees for assisted tax return preparation, selling paid tiers of DIY tax software, collecting franchise royalties (approximately 30% of gross tax preparation revenue from U.S. franchisees), earning fees on consumer financial products distributed with bank partners, and collecting software processing and subscription fees from small business solutions like Wave.

### 4. Who are its two closest publicly traded competitors that compete most directly in the company’s largest revenue source, and what makes this company different from them? Explain why you chose those two.
* **Intuit Inc. (INTU)**: Intuit is HRB's primary competitor in DIY tax preparation through TurboTax and small business software through QuickBooks. HRB differs from Intuit by relying heavily on a physical retail network (~9,000 storefronts) and human tax professionals, whereas Intuit is primarily a digital software provider.
* **Frontdoor, Inc. (FTDR)**: While not a tax preparer, Frontdoor is a direct publicly traded peer in specialized consumer services with a similar recurring service delivery model. HRB differs by having extreme seasonal revenue concentration (February to April tax season) and direct exposure to tax regulatory changes.

*Note on direct tax competitors*: Independent tax preparers and Jackson Hewitt represent primary storefront tax rivals, but Intuit is the main publicly traded direct competitor for individual tax filings.

### 5. Write one sentence I could use to explain this business to a friend with no investing experience.
H&R Block helps people and small businesses file their annual income taxes and access their refund money through thousands of local storefronts and online software.

### 6. What is one common misunderstanding or oversimplification that could lead an investor to analyze this company incorrectly?
A common oversimplification is assuming that growing Earnings Per Share (EPS) reflects a healthy, expanding customer base. In reality, HRB has retired over 40% of its common stock since FY2016 through aggressive share buybacks, which artificially inflates EPS even while total tax return filing volumes remain flat or slowly decline.

### 7. Using the most recent annual report, what are the main business segments of the company, which generates the most revenue, and which generates the most profit? If those are different segments, explain why.
Based on Item 1 of the FY2025 Form 10-K, H&R Block operates and reports as a **single reportable operating segment** encompassing all continuing operations. Because all operations (Assisted Tax, DIY Tax, Financial Products, and Small Business) are aggregated into one segment, separate segment-level revenue and profit breakdowns are not reported in SEC financial statements.

## Business Model Questions

### 1. What is the business model of the company?
HRB operates a high-margin, highly seasonal consumer service and software model. It leverages its national retail brand footprint, proprietary software, and banking partnerships to charge per-return preparation fees and generate financial transaction revenues during the annual tax filing season.

### 2. Is revenue recurring, one-time, or repeat but non-contractual?
**Repeat but non-contractual.** Taxpayers are required by law to file tax returns every year, creating dependable repeat business. However, clients are under no multi-year contract and can easily switch to competitors, automated AI tools, or free government filing options each year.

### 3. Where does pricing power come from?
Pricing power stems from brand trust, taxpayer anxiety regarding IRS audits or costly filing errors, convenience of storefront locations, and immediate access to tax refund loans (Refund Advances) for liquidity-constrained clients.

### 4. What would happen to revenue if their best-selling product was made obsolete by a competitor tomorrow?
Because assisted tax preparation accounts for the vast majority of consolidated revenues (~$3.8 billion in FY2025), obsolescence of its tax filing service would eliminate nearly all of the company's revenue and collapse profitability, as ancillary financial services (Spruce, Emerald Card) rely directly on tax refund distributions.

### 5. Based on disclosures in the most recent annual report, are any customers responsible for more than 10% of revenue? If this is not disclosed in the filing, say "not disclosed" rather than guessing.
**Not disclosed** (no individual consumer or client accounts for 10% or more of total consolidated revenue).

### 6. Based on the most recent annual report, what percentage of revenue comes from the top product or business segment? How has that percentage changed over the last three years?
The company reports 100% of its revenue under a **single reportable segment**. In FY2025, HRB prepared 11.3 million U.S. assisted returns and 3.8 million paid DIY returns. Specific sub-product revenue percentages are not disclosed as distinct reporting segments in the 10-K filings.

### 8. Which segment generates the most profit, and is that different from the one generating the most revenue?
Because HRB operates as a single reporting segment, segment-level profit breakdowns do not exist. Consolidated net income from continuing operations was $609.5 million on $3.8 billion in revenue in FY2025.

## Risk Analysis

### 1. Three Biggest Specific Risks (3–5 Years)
1. **AI & Automated DIY Tax Disruption (Industry-Wide / Gradual)**: Advances in consumer AI models allow taxpayers to handle complex tax scenarios independently at near-zero cost, eroding demand for HRB's paid retail preparers and compressing margins on its ~9,000 storefronts.
2. **Pricing Power Ceiling & Volume Attrition ("Melting Ice Cube Risk") (Company-Specific / Gradual)**: HRB's top-line growth relies almost entirely on raising Net Average Charges per return rather than growing customer volume. Continual price hikes on low-to-middle-income filers risk accelerating customer churn toward free or low-cost digital alternatives.
3. **Regulatory Free-Filing Expansion (Macro / Sudden to Gradual)**: Political attempts to expand free federal/state tax filing portals (such as the proposed Direct File Act of 2026) threaten to remove millions of simple tax filers from HRB's paid customer acquisition funnel.

### 2. Bear Case Presentation
HRB exhibits classic traits of a value trap. While headline EPS figures look strong, EPS has been heavily engineered by repurchasing over 40% of outstanding shares since FY2016, even as underlying Free Cash Flow dropped from $752 million in FY2023 to $598 million in FY2025 and total debt rose to $2.64 billion. The business carries heavy fixed costs across ~9,000 retail locations, competing against software competitors with zero marginal cost. Relying on fee increases to offset flat customer volume is unsustainable. Trading at 12.1x P/E—above its 5-year historical average of 10.6x—the stock does not provide an adequate margin of safety against long-term technological disruption.

## Final Verdict

**BULL CASE**: Magic Formula screening highlights HRB as statistically cheap and high-quality, ranking #9 overall with a high Return on Capital (159.16%), a 12.92% Earnings Yield, stable ~22% operating margins, and a ~5% dividend yield backed by recent quarterly earnings beats.

**BEAR CASE**: Free cash flow has declined 20% over the last two fiscal years ($752M to $598M) while total debt rose to $2.64 billion. Revenue growth is driven almost entirely by price hikes rather than customer volume growth, leaving a heavy 9,000-store retail footprint vulnerable to long-term AI automation and free government tax filing tools.

**Verdict: Watch**

While H&R Block's statistical cheapness and strong return on capital satisfy the initial Magic Formula screening criteria, a deeper look reveals deteriorating free cash flow and a business model reliant on price hikes to cover flat customer volume. The stock is not an immediate "Avoid" due to its powerful cash generation and high capital returns, but it is not a "Buy" given its 12.1x P/E valuation relative to its 10.6x historical average and rising debt levels. Investors should keep HRB on a watchlist to evaluate whether new leadership can stabilize organic volume growth and lower retail overhead before initiating a position.
